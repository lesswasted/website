Beverage packaging: reuse vs recycling — annotated bibliography

Scope: energy and carbon intensity of glass, aluminium, PET and steel/PET keg beverage packaging, with an emphasis on reuse-vs-recycling comparisons and the reconditioning (washing) and transport assumptions that drive the results. Compiled for the Less Wasted keg reuse context.

How to read this. Conclusions in this literature hinge almost entirely on three assumptions: the number of reuse cycles a refillable achieves, the reconditioning (washing) inventory, and the return transport distance. Two studies using the same materials can reach opposite conclusions by varying these. The comparison table below extracts those parameters per study so you can see what’s actually driving each headline. Where a source is industry-funded (PET or aluminium trade associations) it is flagged — those reach opposing conclusions from overlapping data and are best treated as bounding cases rather than neutral evidence.

DOI confidence. DOIs marked ✓ were confirmed from the publisher or an institutional repository. Items given only a ScienceDirect PII or a note should have their DOI verified before formal citation.

A. Cross-material foundational LCAs

Amienyo, D., Gujba, H., Stichnothe, H. & Azapagic, A. (2013). Life cycle environmental impacts of carbonated soft drinks. International Journal of Life Cycle Assessment, 18(1), 77–92. DOI: 10.1007/s11367-012-0459-y ✓

The most-cited reference point in the field. Cradle-to-grave, UK context. Establishes that packaging dominates the footprint (59–77% of most impact categories), that single-use glass has the highest GWP and PET the lowest of the three, and — via sensitivity analysis — that reusing glass roughly three times brings it level with single-use PET and aluminium cans. Independent (academic).

Brock, A. & Williams, I. (2020). Life Cycle Assessment of Beverage Packaging. Detritus, 13, 47–61. (Open access via eprints.soton.ac.uk/447477; verify DOI.)

Broader, more recent comparative ranking across eleven CML impact categories; readable, and useful for the policy framing (single-use taxes as a lever toward reusables). Independent (academic).

B. Glass — reuse vs recycling, with reconditioning inventories

Landi et al. (2020). Reusing glass bottles in Italy: A life cycle assessment evaluation. Procedia CIRP, 90, 192–197. DOI: 10.1016/j.procir.2020.01.094 ✓

Your rotation-threshold anchor. Models a real refillable water system (98.69% return rate, ~1.31% loss per delivery) and reviews European evidence that a refillable beer system at 20 reuses over 250 km delivers roughly −79% climate change and −76% primary energy versus single-use glass. Independent (academic).

Le Féon, S., Gésan-Guiziou, G., Yannou-Le Bris, G., Aubin, J. & Pénicaud, C. (2024). Life cycle assessment based optimization of scenarios of reusable glass bottles using context-specific key parameters. Cleaner Environmental Systems, 2024. ScienceDirect PII S2666789424000631; open access on HAL: hal.inrae.fr/hal-04705714v1 (verify DOI).

The paper to mine for realistic reconditioning numbers. Explicit cleaning inventory of 0.5 L water, 0.008 kg soda ash and 0.01 kWh electricity per bottle, a 1% reject rate at collection, and a 19 t lorry to a cleaning facility 100 km away, across 55 input parameters. Its key contribution: a poorly configured returnable system can come out slightly worse than single-use, correcting the idealised generic assumptions in older work. Independent (academic).

Van Doorsselaer, K. & Lox, F. (1999). LCA comparison between recycle and reuse of glass beer bottles. (ResearchGate 302009172; verify DOI.)

Older but foundational for the beer-bottle reuse-vs-recycle question specifically; models different reuse percentages across the full chain (washing, filling, pasteurising, wastewater). Independent (academic).

C. Aluminium and PET

Niero, M. & Olsen, S.I. (2016). Circular economy: To be or not to be in a closed product loop? A LCA of aluminium cans with inclusion of alloying elements. Resources, Conservation & Recycling. ScienceDirect PII S0921344916301604 (verify DOI).

Explains why the ~5%-of-primary-energy remelting figure for aluminium doesn’t fully close the loop: alloying elements and collection losses mean primary metal keeps entering. Closed-loop can-to-can beats mixed-scrap recycling. Independent (academic).

Tamburini et al. (2021). Plastic (PET) vs bioplastic (PLA) or refillable aluminium bottles — what is the most sustainable choice for drinking water? A life-cycle analysis. Environmental Research, 2021. ScienceDirect PII S0013935121002681 (verify DOI).

Key for the reusable-vs-single-use logic: a single PET bottle has the lowest production impact of any material, but over a year of use the single-use PET footprint balloons while a refillable’s per-year production impact becomes negligible regardless of material — washing method then dominates. Independent (academic).

NAPCOR / Franklin Associates (2023). PET Life Cycle Assessment Report. napcor.com/pet-life-cycle-assessment-report-2023.

Finds single-use PET better than cans and glass for CSDs and still water (US). Industry-funded (PET trade association) — bounding case, not neutral.

The Aluminum Association / John Beath Environmental (2023). Aluminum Cans Leading the Way to Limit Carbon Impact. aluminum.org.

Meta-review putting cans similar to PET and better than glass; reports GWP ranges of 0.11–0.51 (Al can), 0.39–1.05 (glass), 0.11–0.65 (PET) kg CO₂e/L. Industry-funded (aluminium) — bounding case in the opposite direction.

D. Kegs and brewery-specific (most relevant to Less Wasted)

Morgan, D.R., Styles, D. & Lane, E.T. (2022). Packaging choice and coordinated distribution logistics to reduce the environmental footprint of small-scale beer value chains. Journal of Environmental Management, 307, 114591. DOI: 10.1016/j.jenvman.2022.114591 ✓ (open access, CC licence)

The single most on-point paper for you. LCA of seven micro-breweries (footprints 727–1336 g CO₂e/L). Switching single-use glass to reusable glass or cans cut Brewery E’s footprint ~27% each; reusable glass was best for four of seven breweries. Critically: replacing steel kegs with PET kegs increases footprints for short-distance delivery, with savings only in long-distance van scenarios — direct support for keeping a reusable steel keg loop in a short-radius network. Independent (academic), open access.

Marrucci, L. et al. (2024). Identifying the most sustainable beer packaging through a Life Cycle Assessment. Science of the Total Environment, 948, 174941. DOI: 10.1016/j.scitotenv.2024.174941 ✓

Apparent counterpoint to Morgan — finds draught beer in PET kegs ~90% lower footprint than other formats — but note the crucial caveat that all formats in this study are one-way / non-reusable, so it compares a one-way PET keg to one-way glass and cans, not to a reusable steel keg. Its packaging weights (glass ~60.6 kg/hl vs PET keg 1.5, can 5.5) neatly quantify the glass transport penalty. Independent (academic).

From Circular to Linear? Assessing the Life Cycle Environmental and Economic Sustainability of Steel and Plastic Beer Kegs (2022). Circular Economy and Sustainability (Springer). DOI: 10.1007/s43615-021-00142-w ✓

Covers both environmental and cost sides of the steel-vs-plastic keg choice, and notes plastic kegs improve markedly where a local closed-loop PET recycling route exists. Independent (academic).

E. Reviews, meta-analysis and Dutch context

LCA of reusable food and beverage packaging systems: a proposal of good practice (2025). Journal of Cleaner Production. ScienceDirect PII S0959652625005578 (verify DOI).

Methodological guide: identifies number of reuse cycles and reconditioning configuration (washing, refilling, return logistics) as the two central parameters. Use this to decide which assumptions to defend in your own model. Independent (academic).

Zero Waste Europe / Reloop (2020). Reusable vs single-use packaging: a review of environmental impacts (synthesis of 32 LCAs).

Aggregate figures: reusable glass −85% vs single-use glass, −75% vs single-use PET, −57% vs cans; breakeven after 2–3 reuse cycles; transport the dominant reusable hotspot. Advocacy-commissioned grey literature — but aggregates peer-reviewed inputs and its breakeven figure aligns with the academic work.

CE Delft (2018/2021). LCA: recycling of household plastic packaging waste. ce.nl/en/publications/1208.

Dutch policy-grade grey literature (not journal peer-review, but ISO-consistent and the standard NL reference). Concludes that at-source and post-consumer plastic separation plus a deposit on large PET all beat incineration environmentally. CE Delft’s publications library is the place to look for Dutch deposit-return and reuse-system inventories.

Dutch context sources (authoritative-for-NL, not peer-reviewed): Milieu Centraal — packaging glass at 60% recycled content saves only ~15% energy vs new glass, because remelting cullet still needs ~1,300 °C; washing a bottle uses ~600 mL water vs ~2 L to make a new one (Transitieagenda Consumptiegoederen / Mission Reuse pilot). The Dutch reuse loop in practice is essentially the shared beer bottle (Bruine Nederlandse Retourfles), refilled up to ~40× over ~8 years; most other consumer glass goes via the glasbak and is melted, not washed and refilled.

Bottom line for the keg model

The peer-reviewed weight of evidence supports a reusable steel keg in a short-radius local loop over both single-use anything and one-way PET kegs (Morgan et al. 2022 is explicit on the steel-vs-PET-keg point at short distance). The single-use-vs-refillable split matters more than the material: once a refillable clears its rotation threshold (breakeven ~2–3 cycles for glass per ZWE; kegs far exceed this), the container material stops driving the result and washing energy plus return distance take over (Le Féon et al. 2024; Tamburini et al. 2021). Those two variables — washing inventory and collection radius — are the only ones that could erode a keg loop’s advantage, and both are favourable for a tight local café network.
